The three canonical books, honestly compared

CLRS (Cormen, Leiserson, Rivest, Stein, Introduction to Algorithms) is the reference: every proof this course folded into a collapsible layer is there in full, with the pseudocode conventions our steppable implementations quietly mirror. Use it as an encyclopedia — read the chapter after meeting the idea here, not before. Deepest coverage of units 1, 3, 5 and 6 (their recurrences, sorting, graph and DP chapters are definitive).

Skiena (The Algorithm Design Manual) is the practitioner's book — the back-half catalog ("I have a problem that looks like X…") is the professional-grade version of this course's choosing a strategy page, and his war stories are unit 9's spirit in book form. Best first purchase for the audience this course was built for.

Sedgewick & Wayne (Algorithms, 4th ed.) sits between: real, runnable Java for every structure (closest in philosophy to our one-steppable-implementation rule), superb diagrams, lighter on proofs. Their booksite and Coursera course pair well with units 2–5.

The free heavyweight: Jeff Erickson's Algorithms (freely available online) — graduate-level clarity on recursion, DP and graphs, and the best written treatment of unit 11's reductions anywhere. Roughgarden's Algorithms Illuminated series matches this course's intuition-first temperament with the lectures to go with it.

By unit, when one of them grabbed you

Units 1–3 (analysis, structures, sorting): CLRS chapters 1–9 for rigor; Sedgewick for implementation craft; Bentley's Programming Pearls for the engineering aesthetic our counter-driven demos borrow. Units 4–5 (graphs): CLRS's graph chapters for the proofs; Erickson's flow chapters are outstanding and free. Unit 6 (DP & greedy): Erickson again (his DP chapter is the field's best), Kleinberg & Tardos for exchange arguments done beautifully. Unit 7 (strings): Gusfield's Algorithms on Strings, Trees and Sequences — bioinformatics-flavored, definitive. Unit 8 (visualisation): Munzner's Visualization Analysis and Design for the theory; the D3 ecosystem for practice. Unit 9 (systems): Kleppmann's Designing Data-Intensive Applications is the book-shaped version of the whole unit; then primary sources — the HNSW paper (Malkov & Yashunin 2016), the LSM-tree paper (O'Neil et al. 1996), Dynamo (consistent hashing in production, 2007). Unit 11: Garey & Johnson's Computers and Intractability remains the NP-completeness bible (its problem catalog is a professional tool); Mitzenmacher & Upfal for randomization; Vazirani for approximation.

Courses and practice

Lectures: MIT 6.006 and 6.046 (OpenCourseWare, free, superb) map almost unit-for-unit onto this course and its proofs; Princeton's Algorithms I/II (Coursera, Sedgewick himself) onto units 2–5. Practice: LeetCode is drill work — genuinely useful for units 2–7 fluency the way the gym is, and just as unrepresentative of the job; Codeforces/AtCoder if competition sharpens you; Project Euler where math meets code. The honest framing: interview prep is a legitimate, bounded goal — a few weeks of drills on top of this course's understanding beats months of memorization without it.

The reading habit that compounds

The highest-leverage habit isn't a book: it's reading the algorithm sections of the engineering blogs and papers behind systems you use — how Postgres plans joins, how V8 sorts, how Kafka replicates, how FAISS/pgvector implement unit 9's vector search. You now have the vocabulary they assume. That was this course's actual goal: not that you know every algorithm, but that nothing in those documents is a wall anymore.
